Pocket
Lily Potter

As my Bedroom Pop Pantheon was a corollary to Gracie Abrams' *minor*, then this review is a corollary to [that essay](https://mwaterman.dev/music/essays/Bedroom%20Pop%20Pantheon.md). There, I described how the wide-spread popularity of a selection of sad, bedroom-pop adjacent songs made for a *shared solitude* - the feeling of being alone; shared with millions of other listeners. This track is the foil to that array - equally as visceral, with a fraction of the popularity, meaning this solitude is shared only between artist and listener. Although Lily Potter is mainly known for a slew of remixes of [*Oblivion*](https://open.spotify.com/track/73R2oR9LjSe0AyKHSx9S33?si=ee94e301a2f0456d), this is easily her best track. Despite not seeing my life reflected in the lyrics of Pocket, I find it exceptionally tragic and gripping, and I'd like to work out why. 

Sonically, the track is very spacious - leaving room for the listener to feel the impact of each element, and what emotions it evokes. The stripped-back strumming is gentle and articulate, and the slow pace of the track shows the voicing of each string. This sits above a wave sample, also slow, gently rolling in and out. Her vocals come in, wide and whispery, drawn out as to match the instrumental, and the audible air matches the inhale and exhale of the tide. Finally, swirling, soft chimes come in halfway through, subdued under the ukulele. In sum, these elements sound pensive, unobtrusive, and are a fitting supplement to round out the instrumental. Together, they give a rounded, apologetic feel to the track. It feels as if it doesn't want to intrude, and that the gentle feel might be all that she can muster.

This soft instrumental perfectly scaffolds the lyrics, and the story she's trying to tell. They too, are a bit stripped back, with short verses and no chorus, amplifying the confessional feel of the song. I adore the track's namesake line, delivered softly: "I'll hide away // In the pockets of my coat". Even moreso the next couple lines "Nothing to say // The words get caught somewhere between my lips and my throat". The "...and my throat" is so slow, fading to barely a whisper, as if it can barely be said. It perfectly fits the story being told - of words left unsaid - and it feels simultaneously so charged and so painful to deliver. She continues, "you meet me halfway // you see me for all of my mistakes", with long pauses after each line. A torturously slow confession against bright ukulele and gentle chimes. I like the idea that even what we hear might leave something unsaid - that the slow pauses leave space for more than what we hear. Although the track ends on acceptance - "They never bothered you anyway // So I'll meet you in the hallway" - the delivery makes it feel tenuous, uncertain - as meeting in the hallway might be. All to say that the lyrics are immensely gripping, and have me choking up - as if there's words caught between my lips and my throat.

In the general discourse, I think it's easy to write off sad-girl-with-ukulele as trite, or played out, or even as a dime-a-dozen style of music. After coming back to this song Pocket, I just cannot agree. From the intentionally simple composition arises a deeply compelling story, delivered so gently, yet it brings so much weight with it. For this kind of music, I don't think we have to define a song's success by its streaming numbers, or whether mainstream outlets even give it any attention. Instead, it's about one's personal connection to the artist and their art. 

I recently spent some time down the Cape, during which we went for a midnight swim. Staring out across the black infinity of the bay, I listened to this song, wading knee-deep and watching the waves ripple. I don't particularly feel that, in my life, I've left a lot unsaid, or that I need someone to meet me halfway. Nonetheless, I was so moved by this track then. The gentle strums, the matching far-off waves as I literally felt the waves against my legs, and the drawn-out pauses as she whispers out each line. Standing alone, listening to a song from five years ago, with a fraction of the popularity of her other songs, I still felt deeply connected to the artist. Although songs with the popularity of ceilings or Motion Sickness create cultural touchpoints for a generation, the individual experience is just as important. It shows how it is truly between song and listener, and how cathartic it can be to bind music to one's life experiences. That's what it's all about. 

Track Ratings:
Pocket - 100
