Daughter from Hell
Gracie Abrams

I don't want to make myself out as some hopeless romantic who latches onto a sound and can't accept any deviation from it. For years, I followed JPEGMAFIA reinventing his sound on every album, and I loved it every time. It's really an incredible thing when an artist pushes in a new direction, giving you a sound you didn't even know you wanted. This album is not that. It is, instead, a careening disaster. Every day, we get farther from [Good Riddance](https://www.mwaterman.dev/music/Gracie%20Abrams%20-%20Good%20Riddance.md) - not because Gracie's innovating, but because she's actively regressing by trying to be popular. Yes, Gracie Abrams is unfortunately still my favorite living vocalist - she has an impossibly textured whisper that I find to be gripping and intoxicating. As she keeps releasing music, though, it feels as if that's her only redeeming quality. In this review, I'll tear into Daughter From Hell, Gracie's latest album. Although there are high points - even songs I thoroughly enjoy - it's obvious she learned the wrong lesson from the explosive success of That's So True, leaving this album a mess of incoherent, regrettable tracks.

The shame of That's So True is that her worst song became her most popular. From that, she learned that thinly mixed vocalizations of boring lyrics result in billions of streams and millions of dollars. It's not that she is suddenly incapable of writing good songs, it's that she's choosing not to. You show me the incentive, I'll show you the outcome.

She can obviously still make good songs - there really are highlights here. The clear standout is Good Reason !SM, which is a success on all fronts. The guitar is straightforward, but it's pleasantly articulate, and effectively scaffolds her vocals. As always, her voice is exceptional - crisp, breathy, falling in and out of a true whisper. Here, though, she pushes into a high register which sounds excellent. In the past, her voice was composed of layers where only one was falsetto, but here it's clearer and brought higher. As the song progresses - the layering returns, this time filling the soundstage, pushing the width from feeling centered and elevated to spacious across both channels. This arrangement amplifies the impact of the lyrics, with the progression in her tone from "I would be leaving", to "You would start bleeding" to "You would be grieving". The repeated "if only, if only", against the strained, high-pitched delivery is compelling, and solidifies this track as my favorite on the album. 

Humming !SM is another strong track. Although I think the lyrics are a bit too vague to really bite, her fully whispered, downtrodden confession - "What a way to feel in your twenties" is a refreshing reminder of her earlier work. The bridge - "But we're meant to try through humming" - followed by swirling fragments of her voice, eponymously humming - is exceptional. The song is delightful, albeit a bit out of place; I suppose you can hear Justin Vernon too clearly here. Bon Iver's talent for expanded, haunting arrangements, and emotional arcs from lush instrumentation is undeniable, and it's a collaboration I'm glad to see. However - instead of feeling like a welcome digression, it clarifies how bad the surrounding tracks are. It's not that I categorically hate her more poppy tracks - I really didn't mind the second single, Look at My Life !SM. I particularly like the final pre-chorus - "But, oh well, look at my life" is quite engaging. It is reminiscent of [The Bottom](https://mwaterman.dev/music/Gracie%20Abrams%20-%20This%20Is%20What%20It%20Feels%20Like.md?deeplink=The%20Bottom) - a bright, catchy track with ostensibly self-destructive lyrics - an intrinsically interesting contrast. Despite not reaching the quality of The Bottom, I can live with "my nightmare actualized", for now. Even when she's trying for radio play, she can still sound good - so the rest is consciously bad. 

I fear that this popularity-seeking has resulted in carelessness throughout, as the imagery on this album is a complete mess. It's two tired motifs: cars and violence - neither of which she has any clue how to engage with. I absolutely cannot tolerate any more "car as a relationship" - it feels as if it's been played out for decades. Mews !SM - a track that otherwise sounds refined - is rife with these. Engine fading, tires flaming, warning lights, "guess I slowed you down when you planned on racing" - I couldn't care less. Beyond being only partially coherent, it's also trite, painfully so. Quality writing is not a new skill, nor is it new to this genre - Phoebe Bridgers, Lizzy McAlpine, all of Gracie's earlier albums. It's obvious that this imagery is far below par for an artist with this level of resources at her disposal. The experimentation with knives and blood might be even worse - less trite, more pointless. I started fearing this with the first single, Hit The Wall !SM. There's imagery I like - "a room full of doctors and an inkblot" or "hallucinations that I downplay" - but what the hell does she mean by "watch my blade ricochet"? Blades don't ricochet, projectiles do. I get that this is pedantic - but the whole song pivots on "ricochet", her voice escalates as the instrumental tries to soar - so the confusing imagery really hampers the track. It's a three-minute track by one of the biggest artists - we can't pay attention to detail? There are other stumbling points that are similarly tired - all the way to the closer, Cold Goodbyes, with "now the aliens are asking if I'm okay // I'm far out". Imagine that I'm disappointedly shaking my head. I've written at length defending her imagery in the past - my evergreen adoration for the 'forest fire / kerosene' pairing on Full Machine, or the poignant childhood-home details of Right now. But I simply can't do it anymore.

On other songs, we're just running That's So True back. When it released, it was the only time her voice sounded actually bad: stilted delivery of every word with a double-O in it. Now, she sounds even worse. Men Like You !SM is catastrophic - the repeated "you" that's extended to being painful. It sounds as if it's a satire of pop music, an unlistenably astringent soup of you and oohs, and I'm incredulous it made the final cut. It's a shame, because the bridge is amazing - the tightly layered "I remember your face // It got distorted somehow", "Hard to know what to say when we're six feet underground" is moving. Both the lyrics and the sound work well. It has the slightest hint of Hide and Seek in it - the layered vocals packed together - perfectly fitting the ideas of distortion and claustrophobia. Of course, then it is torpedoed by the chorus. The piercing, unpleasant ooh-oohs are a direct consequence - re-running the formula, ruining a track that otherwise had promise. 

Where Men Like You is plain reiteration, Minibar !SM is easily her worst track ever, dethroning That's So True. It's horrifically out of place between the slow-burning Mews and the derealization of Imaginary Friend. Between these two poignant tracks, there's a two-minute intermission to shoot ourselves in the face. In lieu of emotional impact, we get "got fifty bucks and a braincell // someone perceived me, kinda scarred". A reduction from "I'm a forest fire, you're the kerosene" to I'm-just-a-girl-isms that have no place in her discography. The underlying instrumental is soulless, boring, completely flat - devoid of any intrigue. The chorus is an unabashed disaster: "I'm with the girls and the gossipers // Oh, and they're loud, I'm mellow // I'm gone but hello". But hello? Was this song even written by someone who speaks English? It's just syllables: "I'm always subtle, when I'm causing trouble // Hate to burst your bubble". What the fuck are we even talking about? This song truly, utterly, makes no sense to me - it was a continuous lapse in judgement from idea to release. I don't understand who it's for. I worry it'll send me into a fugue state if I have to hear it again. 

Where Minibar infuriates, I'm left exhausted by What If It's Right? !SM, an unwelcome collaboration with - of all people - Marcus Mumford. "What are we even doing?", they ask. Yeah, you said it. I am left with a mix of nausea and apathy. It should go without saying that this song chooses commercial interest over artistic integrity, cashing in to capture a demographic. "In time, funny time, funny boy, funny, I felt like a joke". Some of the clunkiest lyrics ever delivered as if they're serious. At first, I am frustrated - I have to listen to an unharmonious duet where one half is Gracie, and the other half is the spearhead for a decade of terrible stomp-clap-hey. Who asked for this? This soon fades into apathy - why am I even listening to this? I feel that it's just nothing. Nothing of note, at all. Any passion for engaging critically with the rest of the material here has been extinguished.

So where does this leave me? It's been a few months now - I'm through the stages of grief. I can accept that my favorite living vocalist is overly driven by commercial pressure. I started grappling with this idea on my review of her debut EP, [minor](https://mwaterman.dev/music/Gracie%20Abrams%20-%20minor.md), so I suppose I ought to have seen this coming. Still, it hurts. I think that if I had concluded this review while angry at this album, I might have written a dissertation on the implicit contract between artist and listener, and how caving to commercial pressure breaks it. Instead, I'll offer a more personal note. When I wrote my review of Good Riddance, I had already spent nearly two years with the album - having inextricably linked it to my love of winter and gentle memories with my family. I found that the earnest lyrics and beautiful instrumental made it a perfect fit for binding to my life. Listening to that album, I feel there is a tether between me and the music, that it is ensouled. It's clear that's not what this album is for.
